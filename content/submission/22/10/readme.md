    Trees are among the most fascinating life forms on Earth, but what makes them truly fascinating is hiding underground! They are able to break solid rocks with hydraulic roots, all while working together with the underground fungal networks that can stretch for kilometers. Above and below, trees are running biological empires we’re only beginning to understand.


    How does something so massive survive with roots growing so shallow? What are trees really made of and how are they able to grow so big?


    Trees leave no holes in the ground because their immense physical mass comes from harvesting carbon directly out of thin air rather than consuming the surrounding soil. To extract carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, a tree utilizes its crown of leaves to perform photosynthesis. This process combines solar energy, water, and carbon to forge glucose—the primary building block for growth—while releasing oxygen and water vapor back into the air.


    Underground, roots do not ingest soil mass; instead, they function as a precise mining system for water and trace minerals like nitrogen and phosphorus. Guided by sensory caps that detect gravity and moisture, roots spread shallowly outward, using hydraulic pressure to crack rocks and acids to dissolve mineral bonds. To expand their reach, trees form trade alliances with vast underground fungal networks, exchanging canopy-produced sugars for water and distant soil nutrients. Through this combination of atmospheric carbon harvesting and microscopic subterranean mining, massive trees grow to towering heights without creating hollows in the earth below.
