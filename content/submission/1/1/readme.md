  body: >-
    Most life does not live alone. Organisms form symbioses with distantly
    related forms of life, exchanging resources in partnerships that can shape
    entire ecosystems. One of the most widespread examples occurs underground.
    Approximately 70% of plant species partner with arbuscular mycorrhizal
    fungi, which build networks of microscopic threads called hyphae through the
    soil.


    This symbiosis operates through a series of trade deals. Plants provide fungi with large amounts of carbon captured through photosynthesis. In return, fungi explore the soil, acquire nutrients such as phosphorus, and transport them back to their plant partners. Building extensive fungal networks is costly, so fungi must balance the carbon required to construct and maintain them against the benefits of reaching new soil nutrients and plant partners. These individual trade-offs can scale up to influence entire ecosystems, affecting plant nutrition, productivity, and the movement of carbon into soils. Yet the collective outcome of countless individual transactions between plants and fungi across Earth’s soils has remained largely invisible.


    The Mycorrhizal Infrastructure Map visualizes findings from our research published in Science. We combined robotic measurements of fungal networks, thousands of soil samples, machine learning, and global environmental data to predict the abundance of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal hyphae across Earth’s soils. We estimate that these fungi form approximately 110 quadrillion kilometers of hyphae underground, equivalent to roughly one billion times the distance from Earth to the Sun, with a standing biomass roughly six times that of all humans.


    The visualization reveals how microscopic exchanges between plants and fungi scale up to create a globally significant biological infrastructure in Earth’s soils. Together, this work provides a foundation for monitoring plant-fungal symbiosis and identifying where their protection and restoration may have the greatest impact.

      This work bridges years of research across many spatial scales, combining
      meta-analysis of soil cores collected around the world with robotic
      imaging of living fungal networks. Recent advances in robotics allowed
      members of our team to observe how fungal networks grow, transport
      resources, and organize fungal architecture at microscopic scales. This
      work was published in the journal Nature in 2025. We then connected these
      observations with measurements from nearly 16,000 soil samples and
      satellite-derived environmental data, allowing us to move from micron
      scale patterns (10⁻⁶ meters) to global ecosystems (10³–10⁷ meters).


      The resulting global mapping research was published in Science and forms the scientific basis of The Mycorrhizal Infrastructure Map.
